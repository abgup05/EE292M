## 5. Safety nets: what catches a wrong result, what slips past, where the copies are

The hands-on (`Capstone/evidence_desk/docs/safety-nets.md`), each fact read by a command:

- **Copies.** The code has two: this laptop and a private GitHub repository. The record of runs and decisions has, in effect, one: `make backup` writes to the same disk, and `tmutil destinationinfo` says this laptop has no Time Machine destination. Nothing is offline.
- **Keys.** Two API keys in a plain-text `.env`, one of them pasted into a conversation. A GitHub token in the keychain with write access to every repository I have, wider than this project needs. No SSH keys.
- **Ports.** The app listens on `127.0.0.1` only. Two other things on this laptop listen on all interfaces, and I had not known about either.
- **What catches a wrong result:** ten layers, from request validation to the reader's own accept or reject, each with the hole in it written beside it.
- **What slips past all of them:** a wrong stance on a real quote; a passage that retrieval never offered; and reviewers that share the builder's blind spots.

The reviewer's hardest finding here (F1): the note called the new settings file "enforcement". It is five deny rules that match command prefixes. A reordered command, a shell `cat` of the key, a direct `docker` call all get past it.

**Retrieval by hand.** One golden case, traced by hand. The claim: "A handful of buyers account for an outsized slice of sales." The passage that answers it says revenue "is concentrated among a limited number of direct and indirect customers". The claim and the passage share no content word: buyers against customers, slice against concentrated, sales against revenue. Keyword ranking on the claim's words put the passage outside the top fifty. With four queries written by the planner in the filing's vocabulary, it ranked seventh. That one case is the whole measurement in miniature: 7 of 24 with the claim's words, 18 of 24 with the planner's.
